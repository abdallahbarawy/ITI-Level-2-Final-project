THE LAWS OF THE GAME: FOULS AND CARDS

The Laws of the Game are maintained by the International Football Association Board (IFAB) and consist of seventeen laws that govern how football is played and officiated. Two of the most important laws for players concern fouls and misconduct. A foul is committed when a player challenges an opponent in a manner that the referee considers careless, reckless or using excessive force, while the ball is in play. The severity of the challenge determines the punishment. A careless challenge results in a free kick only, a reckless challenge is a free kick plus a caution, and a challenge using excessive force is a free kick plus a sending-off.

Direct free kicks, and penalty kicks when they occur inside the offender's own penalty area, are awarded for physical offences such as kicking, tripping, jumping at, charging, striking or attempting to strike, pushing, and tackling an opponent. They are also given for holding, impeding with contact, biting or spitting at someone, throwing an object at the ball or an opponent, and deliberately handling the ball. Deliberate handball includes making the body unnaturally bigger with the arm or hand, and handling by an attacking player in the build-up to a goal is penalised even if unintentional. Indirect free kicks are awarded for offences such as offside, dangerous play without contact, impeding an opponent without contact, and goalkeeper violations like holding the ball for more than six seconds or handling a deliberate back-pass from a team-mate.

Disciplinary sanctions are shown with cards. A yellow card is a caution and is given for unsporting behaviour, dissent by word or action, persistent infringement of the laws, delaying the restart of play, failing to respect the required distance at a free kick or corner, and entering or leaving the field of play without permission. A red card is a send-off and is shown for serious foul play, violent conduct, spitting, denying the opposing team an obvious goalscoring opportunity by deliberately handling the ball, denying an obvious goalscoring opportunity through a foul, using offensive or abusive language, and receiving a second caution in the same match. A player who receives two yellow cards is shown a red card and leaves the field.

The Video Assistant Referee (VAR) can intervene in four match-changing categories: goals and the build-up to goals, penalty decisions, direct red cards, and mistaken identity. VAR only overturns decisions when there is a clear and obvious error or a serious missed incident. Referees are also instructed to protect players from dangerous challenges, and the interpretation of handball and offside has been refined repeatedly in recent years to make decisions more consistent. Understanding these laws helps players avoid unnecessary cards and helps coaches prepare their teams for how a match may be officiated.
