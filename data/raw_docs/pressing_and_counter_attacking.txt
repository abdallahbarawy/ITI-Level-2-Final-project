PRESSING AND COUNTER-ATTACKING

Pressing and counter-attacking are two of the most important transition strategies in modern football, and although they are often presented as opposites, the best teams use both. Pressing means deliberately closing down the opponent in order to win the ball back, ideally high up the pitch. Counter-attacking means attacking immediately after winning possession, before the opponent has time to reorganise. Together they define how a team manages the moments when possession changes hands, which are known as transitions.

Pressing can be organised in different ways depending on where the team wants to win the ball. A high press pushes the defensive line up and attacks the opposition's build-up, aiming to force mistakes near their goal. A mid-block invites the opponent forward before pressing in the middle third, while a low block concedes space in front of the defence and looks to defend deep. Effective pressing is trigger-based: players react to specific cues such as a back-pass, a loose touch, a pass to a player facing their own goal, or a poor clearance. Gegenpressing, or counter-pressing, is the most aggressive form, where the team tries to win the ball back within seconds of losing it.

Counter-attacking is the natural partner of pressing because it exploits the disorder that follows a turnover. The principles are speed, verticality and decision-making. The first pass after winning the ball should usually go forward, and the team should attack with few touches while the opponent is still out of shape. Fast forwards who can run in behind are especially valuable, and a single well-timed pass can turn defence into a goalscoring opportunity in seconds. Teams that defend in a low block, such as Leicester City in 2015-16 or José Mourinho's great sides, often pair deep defending with devastating counters.

The two approaches place different physical and tactical demands on players. Pressing requires high fitness, collective discipline and a compact shape, and it risks leaving space behind the defensive line if opponents play through it. Counter-attacking requires pace, directness and cool decision-making, but it depends on winning the ball in the first place and can leave a team passive if the opponent dominates possession. Many elite teams combine a high press with counter-attacking intent, so that they both create turnovers and punish them immediately. This is also why rest defence, the positioning of players during an attack, matters so much: it determines whether a team can press the counter or is exposed by it.
