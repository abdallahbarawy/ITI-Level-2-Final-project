THE OFFSIDE RULE

Offside is covered by Law 11 of the Laws of the Game and is one of the most frequently misunderstood rules in football. A player is in an offside position if any part of the head, body or feet is in the opponents' half of the pitch and nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent. The hands and arms are not considered when judging offside, and this applies to all players including the goalkeeper. A player who is level with the second-last opponent, or level with the last two opponents, is not in an offside position. Being in an offside position is not an offence in itself; a player must also become involved in active play before the referee penalises them.

A player in an offside position becomes penalised only if, at the moment the ball is played or touched by a team-mate, they become involved in active play in one of three ways. First, they interfere with play by playing or touching a ball passed or touched by a team-mate. Second, they interfere with an opponent by preventing them from playing the ball, for example by obstructing their line of sight, challenging them for the ball, or making an obvious action that clearly impacts the opponent's ability to play the ball. Third, they gain an advantage by playing a ball that rebounds or is deflected off the goalpost, crossbar, a match official or an opponent, or that comes from a deliberate save by any opponent.

The critical moment for judging offside is when the ball is played by a team-mate, not when the attacker receives it. This is why a striker can time a run from an onside position, sprint past a defender and still be onside when the pass is made. There is no offside offence if a player receives the ball directly from a goal kick, a throw-in or a corner kick. However, offside can be penalised in the second phase of play after any of these restarts. If an opponent deliberately plays the ball, a player in an offside position who then receives it is not penalised, because the deliberate play has reset the phase.

In the modern game, offside decisions are supported by the Video Assistant Referee (VAR) and semi-automated offside technology. These tools use calibrated cameras and, at major tournaments, chip technology inside the ball to determine the exact moment of the pass and the position of players. The margin between onside and offside can be a matter of centimetres, which is why the "clear and obvious" threshold and the drawing of lines have become controversial. Understanding offside is essential for defenders trying to hold a line, attackers timing runs, and coaches designing build-up and set-piece routines.
