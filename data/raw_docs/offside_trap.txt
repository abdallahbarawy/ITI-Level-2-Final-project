THE OFFSIDE TRAP

The offside trap is a defensive tactic in which the defenders deliberately step up the pitch at the precise moment the ball is played towards an attacker, leaving that attacker in an offside position. Rather than chasing the forward, the back line moves forward together as a unit to create an imaginary line that the attacker has already passed. If the trap is timed correctly, the assistant referee raises the flag and the attack is stopped without a tackle being made.

The trap depends entirely on coordination and communication. All defenders must step at the same instant, on the pass rather than before or after it. If one defender fails to step and plays the attacker onside, the trap is sprung and the attacker is through on goal with the rest of the defence out of position. This is why the strategy is often described as high-risk, high-reward. It also requires defenders to read the game, watch the ball and the runner simultaneously, and trust one another completely. A single moment of hesitation can be catastrophic.

Historically, the offside trap is associated with Arrigo Sacchi's AC Milan, whose back four of Franco Baresi, Paolo Maldini, Alessandro Costacurta and Mauro Tassotti perfected the system in the late 1980s and early 1990s. It was also central to the success of Ajax and the Netherlands under Rinus Michels and Johan Cruyff, and to George Graham's Arsenal in England. The trap is closely related to the high defensive line: in order to compress the space in which the trap operates, defenders push up towards the halfway line, which in turn makes the team more compact and better able to press.

The main drawback is the space left behind the defensive line. If the trap fails, or if the attacker times their run well and stays onside, there is often no defender left to cover. Attackers counter the trap by delaying their runs, starting from deep positions, making curved runs to stay level with the last defender, or by having one forward drop short while another runs in behind. Modern offside technology and VAR have made the trap more precise but also more scrutinised, since the difference between a well-timed step and a failed one can be measured in centimetres.
